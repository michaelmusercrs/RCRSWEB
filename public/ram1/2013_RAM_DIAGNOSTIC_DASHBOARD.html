Bottom line

Two real, separate problems — both point to inexpensive fixes.

The oil pressure light is almost certainly a dead sensor circuit, not a blown engine — but that has to be proven with a mechanical gauge before you trust it on a 320k motor. The power loss + idle shake is a cylinder 8 misfire. Work it cheap-to-expensive: a $5 spark plug before anything bigger.

Good news — zero lifter tick No valvetrain tick or knock means the dreaded high-mileage HEMI lifter/cam failure path is off the table. The cylinder 8 misfire is almost certainly ignition or fuel (coil, plug, injector) — the cheap, easy stuff. The data backs this up: the PCM logged 0° knock retard, so it isn't fighting detonation.
Before you spend money The scanner trace logs are encrypted and no VIN/DTC was in the captures. Pull the VIN and stored/pending DTCs straight off the D7 — expect codes in the P052x (oil pressure circuit) and P030x (misfire) families. Engine type is confirmed from the data; year/model/trim came from you, not the data.

1

Oil pressure sensor circuit is dead

High

Evidence: across three separate recordings — the stationary capture and both driving loops, engine running and revved to 4,000 rpm — Oil Pressure reads a flat 0.000, and Oil Pressure Sensor Voltage reads a flat 0.000 V.

A working ratiometric oil pressure sensor never outputs 0.000 V — not even at zero real pressure. A dead-flat 0 V means the PCM sees the signal wire open or shorted to ground. So the dash light is being driven by a sensor/wiring fault, not necessarily real low oil pressure.

The catch Because the sensor is dead, the scanner can't read real oil pressure. On a 320k engine you must confirm it with a mechanical gauge before assuming the pump & bearings are fine.

Fix:

  1. Mechanical oil pressure gauge at the sender port — do this first. Parts stores loan them free. Normal hot idle ≈ 25–45 psi, ~2,000 rpm ≈ 45–75 psi → pressure is fine, just replace the sensor. Low/zero → stop driving it.
  2. Inspect the sensor connector & wiring for corrosion / damage / rodent chew.
  3. Replace the oil pressure sensor — look up the exact Mopar part number by VIN before ordering.

2

Cylinder 8 misfire

High

Evidence: in the dedicated misfire capture, the per-cylinder counters for cylinders 1–7 sit flat at 0.000 — only cylinder 8 logs any misfires. The misfire monitor was actively counting throughout, but the catalyst-damaging counter stayed low — so it's not (yet) the cat-melting kind.

A single weak cylinder is a textbook cause of both the idle vibration and the power loss. With zero lifter tick, this is almost certainly ignition or fuel — not mechanical.

Fix — cheapest to most expensive, stop when you find it:

  1. Swap the ignition coil from #8 to another cylinder. If the misfire follows the coil → bad coil (~$30).
  2. Spark plugs — the 5.7 HEMI has 16 plugs (2 per cylinder). At 320k, if they're old, do all 16. Pull #8's and read them.
  3. Swap / test the #8 fuel injector — if coil and plugs are good.
  4. Compression / leak-down on #8 — final check. With no tick this is unlikely, but it still catches a burnt valve.

3

Bank 2 long-term fuel trim running positive

Medium

Evidence: drive loops B and C logged bank-2 long-term trim at mean +6.6%, peak +16.4%. Positive trim = the PCM adding fuel to compensate for a lean or weak bank — and a weak cylinder 8 (which lives on bank 2) would do exactly that.

Don't over-read it: those captures logged bank-1 short-term and bank-2 long-term — different metrics — so I can't fully prove it's bank-specific. A +6.6% mean is within the normal band on its own; the +16% peak is what's worth a look. Most likely this clears once Issue 2 is fixed.

If it doesn't: check for an intake/vacuum leak on the bank 2 side, and check bank-2 injector flow balance.

Realistic total If it's the common stuff — oil pressure sensor + coil/plugs — budget ~$100–250 in parts and ~3–5 hrs of DIY work, all Easy–Moderate. With zero lifter tick, the expensive engine-internal path is essentially ruled out.
